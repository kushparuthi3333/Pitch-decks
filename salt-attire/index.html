Before the four · the messages that don't arrive

Your account sent 1.35 lakh WhatsApp messages last month. Fewer than one in three were delivered.

42,087 of 1,35,321 arrived — 31.1%. On your two biggest automations, 34,403 of 39,996 failures (86.0%) are Meta's own limit on marketing messages: when a brand writes to the same person too often, Meta simply stops delivering. It is not your templates — 135 are approved and none of the rejected two is in use. Two journeys that fire from your product and home pages sent 29,619 messages and delivered one. Your strongest flow, the add-to-cart reminder, was credited with ₹1.0L last month while delivering only 28.3% of what it sent.

₹21,000 / month

If the add-to-cart reminder delivered 40% of its 14,039 sends — the bottom of the range marketing messages normally reach — about 1,650 more messages would arrive a month. At the ₹25 or so each delivered message already earns it, that is about ₹42,000. We bank half, ₹21,000, because credited revenue is not all revenue you would otherwise have lost. Sending fewer messages to the right people is how delivery goes up — not sending more.

Finding 1 · the customers drifting away

6,931 of your customers sit in your own at-risk segments, and there is no win-back to catch them.

Your list holds 80,607 customer profiles. Of the 17,182 your account has scored, 825 are champions and 6,931 are at risk — 6,815 of them reachable on WhatsApp. There is no win-back journey and no welcome journey; what goes out instead are broadcasts.

On 25 September the same flash sale went out two ways. Sent to every customer — 49,010 messages — it brought back 3 orders, 65 paise a message. Sent to 1,715 repeat buyers, it brought back 4, ₹7.22 a message: eleven times as much. Your list responds when it is spoken to as a list of people, and one broad flash-sale template accounted for 47 of last month's 59 unsubscribes. Reachable is not the same as opted in, and we will build consent properly before anything is sent.

₹32,000 / month

If one targeted message a month to the 6,815 converts at the rate your own repeat-buyer send already did — 4 orders from 1,715, about one in 430 — that is about 16 orders at your ₹4,017 basket, roughly ₹64,000. We bank half, ₹32,000, because only customers who have agreed to hear from you can be messaged.

Finding 2 · the sizes that aren't there

92 of your listings have neither an M nor an L in stock — and the gaps sit on your best-sellers.

Across 417 sized listings, only 118 can be bought in every size, and 142 are down to one or two. By size, L is available on 61.4% of its variants and XXL on 46.7%. The thinnest stretch is your best-sellers ranked 26 to 150, where only 57.4% of size variants can be bought. Your #1 and #2 — the Stalwart shawl-collar dress in black and maroon — are in stock in S to XL; further down the list, a shopper who wants an M often finds it gone.

This is the one finding we cannot measure precisely from outside: the storefront shows what is sold out, not how many shoppers left because of it, and some of these will be deliberate end-of-line pieces. That is a question for your team, not a criticism — and a weekly size read of the top 150 is cheap to run.

₹22,000 / month

If keeping the core sizes in stock on the styles that sell wins back one order in a hundred — our assumption, and a deliberately small one — that is about 11 orders a month at your ₹4,017 basket, roughly ₹44,000. We bank half, ₹22,000, because some of those shoppers would have bought another size or another style anyway.

Finding 3 · the payment page

One shopper in four reaches your payment page and chooses nothing.

In the week to 13 October, 341 shoppers reached the payment page and 94 left without selecting a method — 27.6%; the week before it was 120 of 408. Every other step barely leaks: 345 reached the address step and 341 got through it. And once a method is chosen, it works — 54 of 54 cash selections and 172 of 193 prepaid ones became orders, with UPI succeeding 97.8% of the time.

So this is not a gateway problem. It is the moment the total, the fee and the offer appear together: an ₹80 cash fee, a banner that promises 5% off for paying online, and a discount that applies 4%. The levers are what that screen says, in what order, and a reason to finish for someone already reaching for the back button.

₹20,000 / month

At that rate, about 400 shoppers a month leave the payment page without choosing. One in twenty of them finishing — our assumption — is about 20 orders at your ₹4,017 basket, roughly ₹80,000. We bank a quarter, ₹20,000, because some of them come back on their own.

Finding 4 · one fee for every risk level

Your riskiest cash carts pay the same ₹80 as your safest — and return their parcels six times as often.

In August your risk engine flagged 197 orders as high-risk; 99 of them were cash on delivery, and 33.8% of those cash orders came back, against 5.6% in the low-risk band. On our arithmetic that band was about 16% of August's orders and close to half of its returned parcels. Among new customers, high-risk orders return at 19.2% against 1.6% for low-risk.

Three rules exist in your account: one blocks cash on gift cards, one blocks 29 phone numbers and has caught one checkout in fourteen months, and one adds ₹80 to every cash cart — 2% of your basket, whatever the risk. Overall your returns are moderate — 5.3% of August's orders — but courier outcomes reached GoKwik for only 65.7% of them, so every return figure here is a floor, not a measurement.

₹24,000 / month

On August's numbers, high and medium risk together ran 218 cash orders, of which about 58 came back. If a part-payment or a higher fee on those bands turns one in four of them into an order that stays delivered — our assumption — that is about 14 orders at your ₹3,468 cash basket, roughly ₹49,000. We bank half, ₹24,000, because some shoppers will abandon rather than pay upfront. We deliberately quote no return-rate improvement percentage — the tracking does not yet support one.
